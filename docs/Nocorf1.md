This section scrutinizes each generation method, its scientific basis, and the stated output figures.  
2.1. Bio-Photoelectrochemical (BPEC) System  
Mechanism: This technology, often called a photosynthetic microbial fuel cell, aims to directly intercept electrons generated during the water-splitting process (photosystem II) of photosynthesis before they are used by the plant for biomass creation.  
Stated Output: 1.86 \\\\text{ kW} peak power, yielding 7.8 \\\\text{ kWh/day} (based on 4.2 peak sun hours in Michigan). Winter capacity is estimated at 70%.  
Scientific Context: BPEC is an emerging field with significant potential but currently faces challenges in efficiency and long-term stability. A solar conversion efficiency of 0.3%, as noted, is at the higher end of what is demonstrated in laboratory settings for plant-integrated systems. A 2018 review in Biotechnology Advances notes that power densities for such systems are often measured in milliwatts per square meter (mW/m^2) \[1\]. Achieving a peak of 1.86 \\\\text{ kW} (1860 \\\\text{ W}) from a 4,000 \\\\text{ ft}^2 (\\\~372 \\\\text{ m}^2) area would require a power density of \\\~5 \\\\text{ W/m}^2, which is substantially higher than current documented achievements. This figure requires strong justification or represents a reliance on future technological breakthroughs.  
2.2. Microbial Fuel Cell (MFC) System  
Mechanism: This is a more established technology. Heterotrophic bacteria in the anaerobic root zone (rhizosphere) consume organic compounds (exudates) released by the plant roots. In doing so, they release electrons, which are captured by an anode embedded in the medium, flow through an external circuit to do work, and are then combined with an electron acceptor (typically oxygen) at the cathode.  
Stated Output: 1.86 \\\\text{ kW} continuous power, yielding 44.6 \\\\text{ kWh/day}. Winter capacity is 90%.  
Scientific Context: This system is correctly identified as the workhorse due to its 24/7 operation. The key variable is power density (W/m^2). Research on plant-MFCs often reports power densities in the range of 50-300 \\\\text{ mW/m}^2 \[2\]. To achieve 1860 \\\\text{ W} over 372 \\\\text{ m}^2, a power density of \\\~5 \\\\text{ W/m}^2 (5000 \\\\text{ mW/m}^2) is required. This is approximately 15-100 times higher than commonly reported figures in scientific literature. This is the most significant assumption in the entire proposal and must be the primary focus of technical validation.  
2.3. Biological Cooling (Evapotranspiration)  
Mechanism: Trees act as natural air conditioners by moving water from the roots to the leaves, where it evaporates. This phase change from liquid to gas (latent heat of vaporization) absorbs a significant amount of heat energy from the surrounding air.  
Stated Value: Energy savings equivalent to 1.86 \\\\text{ kW} of conventional AC, resulting in 22.3 \\\\text{ kWh/day} of avoided consumption during daylight hours.  
Scientific Context: This is a proven and effective passive cooling strategy. The quantification is plausible. A mature tree can transpire over 100 gallons of water a day, providing a cooling effect equivalent to several room-sized air conditioners. However, it is critical to classify this correctly: This is demand-side reduction, not supply-side generation. It lowers the 'HVAC' number in the consumption analysis; it does not add kilowatt-hours to the generation total.  
2.4. Piezoelectric Generation  
Mechanism: Piezoelectric materials generate a small electric charge in response to applied mechanical stress. In this context, the stress comes from the movement of plants due to wind or internal tropisms.  
Stated Output: 0.01 \\\\text{ kW} (10 \\\\text{ W}) power, yielding 0.08 \\\\text{ kWh/day}. Winter capacity at 80%.  
Scientific Context: Your assessment of this as a "modest" contributor is accurate. Research into energy harvesting from plant motion confirms that the power output is very low, often in the microwatt to milliwatt range per device \[3\]. The stated output is plausible as an aggregate from a large-scale installation but remains a minor part of the overall energy budget.
